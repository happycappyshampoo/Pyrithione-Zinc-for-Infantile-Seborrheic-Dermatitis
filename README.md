# Pyrithione Zinc for Infantile Seborrheic Dermatitis (Cradle Cap): Mechanistic Rationale, Clinical Evidence, and Pediatric Evidence Gaps

A mechanism of action and narrative evidence review of pyrithione zinc (zinc pyrithione) as a topical treatment for infantile seborrheic dermatitis and cradle cap, separating direct infant evidence from adult data, laboratory data, exposure modeling, and regulatory context.

## Abstract

Pyrithione zinc (zinc pyrithione, ZnPT) is an established topical active ingredient for dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis. Laboratory studies show it acts against Malassezia yeasts, and randomized clinical trials show it works in adult scalp disease. These findings give a reason to study it in infantile seborrheic dermatitis (ISD), commonly called cradle cap when it affects the scalp. How much Malassezia contributes to ISD is still not fully defined. Direct infant studies of efficacy and pharmacokinetics for zinc pyrithione remain limited.

The 2019 Cochrane systematic review of ISD treatments found only six randomized trials, rated the certainty of evidence as very low for every comparison, and found no eligible trials of commonly used antifungal treatments. The review shows how weak the infant treatment evidence is overall, but it does not evaluate zinc pyrithione directly (Victoire et al., 2019).

Outside the infant population, human exposure studies and probabilistic modeling indicate very low systemic exposure to zinc pyrithione under studied rinse off conditions. An aggregate exposure model estimated internal exposure of 0.01 to 1.29 micrograms/kg/day among 95th percentile users. A more intensive experimental regimen, daily 2% zinc pyrithione shampoo plus a leave on zinc pyrithione tonic, also produced systemic exposure in the low micrograms/kg/day range (Tozer et al., 2015; Mangion et al., 2021).

These data do not establish infant pharmacokinetics or infant safety. This narrative review brings together mechanistic, adult clinical, topical exposure, toxicologic, regulatory, and pediatric evidence, and it marks clearly where evidence is direct and where it is extrapolated. The evidence supports a biologically plausible and experimentally supported reason to run infant studies. Dedicated studies are still needed to establish efficacy, pharmacokinetics, and safety in infants.

**Keywords:** infantile seborrheic dermatitis; cradle cap; zinc pyrithione; pyrithione zinc; Malassezia; pediatric dermatology; medicated shampoo

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Presentation and Prevalence of Infantile Seborrheic Dermatitis

ISD is a common, generally benign, self limited inflammatory skin condition with redness and greasy or adherent scale. When it affects the scalp it is called cradle cap. It can also involve the eyebrows and face, behind the ears, neck folds, armpits, groin, and other skin folds.

ISD is strongly tied to age. An Australian population study found seborrheic dermatitis in 71.7% of infants younger than three months and 44.5% of children younger than one year, with prevalence dropping substantially afterward. Most cases were minimal or mild (Foley et al., 2003). Most cases improve on their own and resolve within the first year of life (Poindexter et al., 2009; Victoire et al., 2019).

### 1.2 Classification, Scope, and Literature Identification

This paper is a mechanism of action and narrative evidence review. It covers the biological rationale, clinical efficacy evidence, topical absorption and exposure evidence, toxicologic and regulatory context, and pediatric evidence gaps relevant to zinc pyrithione as a possible topical treatment for ISD. It does not evaluate any single commercial formulation.

Literature searches were conducted on September 4, 2026, using PubMed/MEDLINE and the Cochrane Library, supplemented by official U.S. Food and Drug Administration and European regulatory sources and a review of the references cited by key studies and reviews. Search concepts included zinc pyrithione, pyrithione zinc, infantile seborrheic dermatitis, cradle cap, Malassezia, dandruff, oleic acid, lipase, percutaneous absorption, pharmacokinetics, systemic exposure, reproductive toxicity, and developmental toxicity.

Evidence was interpreted by population and by type. Infant evidence was kept separate from adult evidence. Laboratory and microbiology evidence was kept separate from clinical outcome evidence. Exposure and pharmacokinetic studies were kept separate from toxicology studies. Regulatory hazard classifications were kept separate from exposure based risk assessments. When a secondary source made a claim about a specific ingredient, the underlying citation was checked, where available, to see whether it directly supported the ingredient, population, route of exposure, and outcome being discussed.

This was not a systematic review. There was no prospective protocol, PRISMA methodology, formal meta analysis, or independent risk of bias assessment. Statements about absence of evidence refer only to evidence found in this review and are not proof that no other published or unpublished evidence exists.

## 2. Background: Pathophysiology and Current Management of ISD

### 2.1 Etiology and Anatomical Distribution

The cause of ISD is not fully understood and is likely multifactorial. Proposed contributors include oil gland activity, skin barrier function, the body's inflammatory response, lipid composition, and interactions with the skin microbiome, including Malassezia species. Current reviews emphasize both similarities and important differences between infantile and adult seborrheic dermatitis (Borda & Wikramanayake, 2015; Rau et al., 2024).

In adult dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis, Malassezia enzymes called lipases break down skin oil triglycerides and release free fatty acids, including oleic acid. In susceptible people these can disturb the skin barrier and cause flaking. Applying oleic acid to the scalp has produced dandruff like flaking in susceptible people but not in unaffected controls, which points to individual susceptibility as an important factor (DeAngelis et al., 2005; Dawson, 2007; Borda & Wikramanayake, 2015).

This mechanism is well supported in adult dandruff biology but should not be assumed to explain infant disease in the same way. Current ISD reviews still list oil gland activity, lipid abnormalities, skin barrier function, microbiome composition, and immune responsiveness as candidate contributors, and they stress that the relative importance of each is uncertain (Rau et al., 2024).

The distribution of ISD fits involvement of oil gland rich areas and skin folds. Scalp disease is cradle cap, but redness and scale can also appear around the eyebrows, ears, face, neck folds, armpits, groin, and other folds.

### 2.2 Current Topical Management and Antiseborrheic Shampoos

Topical care is the mainstay of managing pediatric seborrheic dermatitis. Depending on severity, location, and persistence, it may include gentle cleansing, softening and mechanical removal of scale, antifungal therapy, and short courses of low potency topical corticosteroids for inflamed skin (Poindexter et al., 2009; Cheong et al., 2016; Chiriac & Wollina, 2024).

Pediatric clinical reviews list shampoos containing zinc pyrithione or selenium disulfide among topical options for scalp seborrheic dermatitis and dandruff (Chiriac & Wollina, 2024). This shows recognized clinical use. It is not evidence from dedicated randomized infant trials of zinc pyrithione. Inclusion in clinical guidance sets the treatment context but does not establish infant efficacy or safety.

### 2.3 Regulatory Context

In the United States, pyrithione zinc is included in FDA OTC Monograph M032 as an active ingredient for controlling seborrheic dermatitis at concentrations of 0.95% to 2% for brief exposure rinse off products and 0.1% to 0.25% for leave on products (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2021). This sets the conditions under which an appropriately formulated and labeled over the counter product may be marketed for seborrheic dermatitis. It is not infant specific trial evidence and does not create a separate cradle cap indication.

European regulation of zinc pyrithione shows the difference between a hazard classification and an exposure based risk assessment. Zinc pyrithione was harmonized under the EU Classification, Labelling and Packaging framework as a Category 1B reproductive toxicant for developmental toxicity (H360D), based mainly on developmental effects seen in animal studies after systemic exposure. That hazard classification does not by itself establish risk from a particular topical use. The Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety (SCCS) separately evaluated consumer exposure and concluded that zinc pyrithione was safe as an anti dandruff ingredient in rinse off hair products at concentrations up to 1% under the conditions assessed (SCCS, 2020).

Under EU cosmetics law, a Category 1A or 1B reproductive toxicant can remain in cosmetics only by exception, and every legal criterion for that exception must be met. The SCCS safety criterion was met, but it was not established that no suitable alternative anti dandruff ingredients were available. Zinc pyrithione was therefore added to the EU list of substances prohibited in cosmetic products. The prohibition should not be read as an SCCS finding that the evaluated 1% rinse off exposure was unsafe (SCCS, 2020; European Commission, 2021).

## 3. Mechanism of Action of Zinc Pyrithione

### 3.1 Cellular and Molecular Mechanism

Zinc pyrithione acts against Malassezia partly by disrupting the balance of metals inside the cell and the iron sulfur dependent metabolism that cells rely on. Malassezia globosa and M. restricta are among the main Malassezia species associated with dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis, although their abundance and importance vary by population, body site, and study method (Dawson, 2007).

Reeder et al. (2011) showed that zinc pyrithione raised intracellular copper and impaired iron sulfur cluster containing proteins in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. They also showed zinc pyrithione driven copper accumulation and growth inhibition in M. globosa, supporting a copper dependent mechanism in that species.

Park et al. (2018), studying M. restricta directly, saw a different metal response. Zinc pyrithione caused a marked dose dependent rise in intracellular zinc, along with disruption of iron sulfur cluster biosynthesis and mitochondrial function. Gene expression analysis also showed reduced activity of multiple lipase genes, including lipases involved in how the organism interacts with the lipid rich scalp environment (Park et al., 2018).

Taken together, these findings point to a multifactorial and possibly species dependent mechanism: altered metal balance, disrupted iron sulfur dependent metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, and effects on lipid use, rather than a single universal mechanism that works the same way in every Malassezia species.

### 3.2 Targeted Delivery to the Scalp Surface and Follicular Infundibulum

How well a rinse off antifungal works depends not only on its intrinsic activity but also on whether it deposits and stays at the right skin sites. Zinc pyrithione dissolves poorly in water, which favors deposition of particles on the skin after rinsing. Malassezia lives both on the scalp surface and in the follicle, especially the upper follicular opening (the infundibulum).

Delivery studies summarized by Mangion et al. show zinc pyrithione depositing on the scalp and inside follicular structures, which offers a plausible explanation for lasting local antifungal activity after a rinse off application (Mangion et al., 2021).

Local deposition should not be confused with zero systemic absorption. Skin absorption has been measured quantitatively and is covered separately below.

### 3.3 Clinical Corroboration in Adult Populations

Human studies support the antifungal activity and clinical relevance of zinc pyrithione, although they do not directly prove that every intracellular mechanism seen in the laboratory happens the same way in people.

Leong et al. (2021) showed that low concentrations of zinc pyrithione inhibit Malassezia in vitro and documented a measurable drop in scalp M. restricta after zinc pyrithione shampoo in a longitudinal human study. This is direct evidence that topical zinc pyrithione can reduce Malassezia on the human scalp (Leong et al., 2021).

Randomized clinical evidence also supports meaningful activity in adult dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis. Piérard-Franchimont et al. (2002) compared 1% zinc pyrithione shampoo with 2% ketoconazole shampoo in 331 eligible subjects with severe dandruff or seborrheic dermatitis. Both produced substantial improvement after four weeks: total dandruff severity improved by about 67% with zinc pyrithione and 73% with ketoconazole. Ketoconazole was statistically superior and was associated with lower recurrence during untreated follow up (Piérard-Franchimont et al., 2002).

These results matter because they show clinical efficacy of zinc pyrithione without requiring the conclusion that it is the most effective antifungal. Schwartz et al. (2013) also showed clinical efficacy of zinc pyrithione based anti dandruff formulations in randomized adult testing (Schwartz et al., 2013).

Together these data support a path from laboratory antifungal activity to reduced scalp Malassezia and clinical benefit in adult scalp disease. They do not establish efficacy in infants.

## 4. The Infant Clinical Trial and Exposure Evidence Base

### 4.1 Systematic Review Evidence and the Scope of the Infant Evidence Gap

The 2019 Cochrane systematic review by Victoire et al. is the main systematic assessment of the ISD treatment literature. It used standard Cochrane methods. Its main limitation was not the review process but the small and methodologically weak set of studies available to include.

The review found six randomized controlled trials involving 310 randomized children, with 297 included in the analysis. Trials were short, from 10 to 42 days, and tested a mix of different interventions. Certainty of evidence was rated very low for every comparison because of risk of bias, imprecision, indirectness, inconsistent outcome measures, and poor trial reporting (Victoire et al., 2019).

Importantly, no eligible trials evaluated commonly used antifungal treatments. Cochrane named antifungals, shampoos and brushing, emollients, corticosteroids, and other commonly recommended interventions as priorities for adequately powered controlled studies (Victoire et al., 2019).

A targeted PubMed search run for this review, combining zinc pyrithione terms with infantile seborrheic dermatitis and cradle cap, returned no records. A zero result does not prove that no relevant study exists, so it is read together with the Cochrane review and the wider pediatric literature rather than as a standalone conclusion.

The Cochrane review therefore shows the breadth and weakness of the infant treatment evidence base but does not directly test zinc pyrithione. Its findings should not be read as evidence for or against infant efficacy or safety of zinc pyrithione.

### 4.2 Ingredient Specific Systemic Exposure and Safety Evidence

Some secondary clinical literature applies general caution to several antiseborrheic or keratolytic shampoo ingredients in newborns. Micali et al. (2021), for example, group salicylic acid, sulfur, selenium, and zinc pyrithione together and state that these shampoos are generally not recommended because of possible systemic absorption (Micali et al., 2021).

Checking the cited evidence shows why ingredient level precision matters. Micali et al. cite the consensus guidance of Cheong et al. (2016). In the infant treatment section of that source, the explicit evidence on absence of systemic absorption concerns ketoconazole shampoo, based on a small study of 13 infants. Cheong et al. do not provide comparable infant pharmacokinetic data for zinc pyrithione (Cheong et al., 2016; Brodell et al., 1998).

This does not show that general caution about zinc pyrithione is wrong. It shows a narrower point: the cited source does not itself demonstrate clinically meaningful systemic absorption of zinc pyrithione in infants.

Available human exposure studies and probabilistic modeling indicate very low systemic exposure to zinc pyrithione under studied rinse off conditions. In a probabilistic aggregate exposure model that accounted for amount of product used, frequency of use, use of multiple rinse off products, and market share, estimated internal exposure among 95th percentile users ranged from 0.01 to 1.29 micrograms/kg/day (Tozer et al., 2015).

Mangion et al. (2021) summarize a deliberately intensive human exposure regimen: daily use of 2% zinc pyrithione shampoo plus a 0.25% leave on zinc pyrithione tonic for four days. The highest reported systemic exposure was 4.38 micrograms/kg/day. These are adult exposure data and should not be read as infant pharmacokinetics.

For toxicologic context, 0.5 mg/kg/day, equal to 500 micrograms/kg/day, was used as a toxicologic point of departure in SCCS risk assessment (SCCS, 2018). The top of the modeled 95th percentile consumer exposure, 1.29 micrograms/kg/day, is about 388 times lower than that value. The intensive 4.38 micrograms/kg/day exposure described by Mangion is more than 100 times lower. These comparisons give context only and are not formal infant safety margins.

The SCCS human exposure assessment independently supports low skin uptake. For risk assessment, SCCS used a conservative skin absorption assumption of 1%. The supporting human study found that up to 0.22% of the applied zinc pyrithione was recovered in urine after combined shampoo and leave on exposure. After accounting for unmeasured later excretion and possible tissue retention, SCCS concluded total absorption was most probably not greater than 1% (SCCS, 2020).

The SCCS also noted that earlier topical reproductive studies showed no reproductive effects at the highest skin doses tested and concluded that zinc pyrithione was unlikely to be a concern for fertility, while acknowledging developmental effects in newer animal studies of systemic exposure (SCCS, 2020). These observations give useful context about route of exposure but do not replace dedicated infant safety studies.

Taken together, the exposure literature supports a more precise conclusion than either "zinc pyrithione is not absorbed" or "systemic absorption in infants is established." Quantitative studies show limited systemic exposure under studied adult and experimental conditions. What remains uncharacterized is whether infant skin characteristics, body surface area to mass ratio, barrier integrity, treated surface area, formulation, and use pattern meaningfully change that exposure after clinically relevant brief exposure rinse off application.

### 4.3 Net Assessment

Dedicated infant randomized efficacy trials and pharmacokinetic studies of zinc pyrithione remain insufficient.

The available literature therefore neither establishes clinically meaningful systemic exposure to zinc pyrithione in infants nor rules it out. This is a population specific evidence gap, not evidence in itself of a zinc pyrithione specific systemic hazard.

This distinction matters when zinc pyrithione is discussed next to compounds with different absorption and toxicity profiles. Salicylic acid, for example, has documented systemic salicylate toxicity after excessive topical exposure in infants (Oualha et al., 2012). Evidence about one antiseborrheic ingredient should not automatically be applied to another just because both are used for scaling conditions.

Adult and ex vivo zinc pyrithione data showing very low but measurable skin exposure (Rush et al., 2019) are relevant to biological plausibility, risk assessment, and future study design. They cannot establish infant efficacy or safety.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Efficacy Claims Rest on Mechanism and Adult Population Data

The clinical rationale for investigating zinc pyrithione in ISD rests on mechanistic evidence against Malassezia, targeted scalp delivery data, reduced Malassezia on the human scalp in clinical studies, and randomized adult efficacy data.

Direct infant randomized efficacy evidence remains inadequate. The lack of dedicated infant zinc pyrithione trials is an ingredient specific limitation, but it sits within a broader ISD treatment literature in which many commonly recommended interventions have also not been adequately tested in randomized controlled trials.

The appropriate inference is not that adult data establish infant efficacy. It is that adult and mechanistic evidence give a rational basis for running direct infant studies.

### 5.2 Ingredient Level Precision Matters in Safety Discussion

Safety discussions about topical therapy in infants should distinguish between compounds rather than treating antiseborrheic ingredients as interchangeable.

For zinc pyrithione, quantitative adult and ex vivo evidence shows low systemic exposure under studied topical conditions. Secondary sources that group zinc pyrithione with salicylic acid, sulfur, or selenium under general absorption cautions should not be read as showing equivalent pharmacokinetics or equivalent evidence of systemic toxicity for each ingredient.

At the same time, the absence of identified infant zinc pyrithione toxicity should not be turned into a positive claim of infant safety. Population appropriate safety evidence is needed to define risk definitively.

### 5.3 The Role of Mechanism and Evidence Reviews Given Limited Pediatric Trial Data

When high quality pediatric randomized trials are scarce, mechanism of action and evidence reviews serve a distinct purpose. They clarify which conclusions come from direct pediatric evidence, which are supported by adult clinical studies, which come from experimental biology or exposure modeling, and which remain extrapolations.

This hierarchy matters especially in ISD because the condition is common, usually mild, and self limited. Because it tends to resolve on its own, it is hard to tell clinical improvement without a control group from a real treatment effect.

Mechanistic evidence does not replace an infant randomized controlled trial. Its value is in establishing biological coherence, identifying plausible therapeutic targets, informing dose and exposure questions, and helping decide which interventions deserve population specific clinical study.

### 5.4 Limitations

This narrative review is limited mainly by the scarcity of direct infant evidence on zinc pyrithione.

Much of the mechanistic evidence comes from in vitro or experimental models, and much of the clinical evidence comes from adult scalp disease. The relationship between Malassezia and ISD is still not fully defined, and infantile and adult seborrheic dermatitis should not be assumed to have identical causes.

The available pharmacokinetic and exposure evidence shows very low systemic exposure under studied conditions outside infancy. Differences in infant skin anatomy, body surface area to mass ratio, barrier integrity, formulation, disease severity, treated surface area, and frequency of application limit direct extrapolation.

This review did not use a systematic review protocol, prospective registration, formal PRISMA methodology, or independent risk of bias assessment. Statements about absence of evidence refer to evidence found through the searches and citation review described above, not proof that no other data exist.

Several important zinc pyrithione studies have direct or indirect industry involvement. This does not invalidate their findings but is relevant to interpreting the total evidence. The author of this review also has a financial interest in a company that markets a zinc pyrithione containing product, as disclosed below.

These limitations constrain conclusions about infant efficacy, pharmacokinetics, and safety.

## 6. Research Priorities and Future Directions

### 6.1 Identified Priorities for Controlled Investigation

* Randomized controlled trials of brief exposure rinse off zinc pyrithione formulations as standalone treatment in infants with ISD, using standardized and validated severity measures
* Formal infant pharmacokinetic assessment after clinically relevant rinse off application, including effects of treated surface area, barrier integrity, contact time, frequency, and formulation
* Comparative trials against commonly used approaches, including gentle cleansing and scale removal, topical azole antifungals, and appropriately selected anti inflammatory therapies
* Evaluation of time to improvement, complete resolution, recurrence, and any need for maintenance therapy
* Prospective reporting of local adverse effects, systemic exposure, treatment discontinuation, and caregiver acceptability
* Trial designs able to separate treatment associated improvement from spontaneous resolution, given the favorable natural history of ISD

These priorities match Cochrane's broader call for better trials of commonly recommended ISD interventions, including shampoos and brushing, antifungal agents, corticosteroids, and emollient approaches (Victoire et al., 2019).

### 6.2 Research Gap and Need for Population Specific Evidence

The lack of dedicated infant randomized trials and pharmacokinetic studies identifies a clear need for population specific investigation.

Existing molecular, microbiological, delivery, exposure, and adult clinical evidence provides a substantial mechanistic rationale for such studies. What remains unresolved is whether efficacy and exposure characteristics established or inferred in non infant settings carry over to infants at the concentrations, contact times, formulations, treated surface areas, and use patterns relevant to ISD.

The central research question is therefore no longer simply whether zinc pyrithione has antifungal activity against Malassezia. That is well supported. The clinically relevant questions are whether this activity produces meaningful benefit in infantile seborrheic dermatitis, what systemic exposure occurs in infants under realistic rinse off conditions, and how the benefit and risk profile compares with alternative management approaches.

## 7. Conclusion

### 7.1 Mechanism Summary and Clinical Rationale

Zinc pyrithione has a well supported antifungal mechanism against Malassezia species, involving disruption of intracellular metal balance and iron sulfur dependent metabolism, with additional effects on mitochondrial function and lipid use. Findings differ somewhat between M. globosa and M. restricta, supporting complementary and possibly species dependent mechanisms rather than a single universal pathway (Reeder et al., 2011; Park et al., 2018).

Delivery studies support deposition of zinc pyrithione at scalp and follicular sites where Malassezia lives. Human studies show reductions in Malassezia after zinc pyrithione shampoo use, and randomized adult studies show therapeutic benefit in dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis.

Extending these findings to ISD is biologically plausible but remains inferential. The precise role of Malassezia in infant disease is still incompletely defined, and dedicated infant efficacy trials are lacking.

Adult human exposure studies, human skin experiments, probabilistic modeling, and regulatory risk assessments consistently indicate low systemic exposure under studied topical conditions. These data inform the risk question but cannot characterize infant pharmacokinetics directly.

The available evidence therefore supports a conclusion of infant specific uncertainty, rather than either established infant safety or demonstrated zinc pyrithione specific systemic toxicity.

### 7.2 Next Steps for Clinical Evaluation

The 2019 Cochrane review shows that the broader evidence base for ISD treatment is small and of very low certainty and did not identify randomized trials of commonly used antifungal therapies. This is best understood as an unresolved treatment evidence gap, not evidence against zinc pyrithione specifically.

Molecular and adult clinical evidence give a rational basis for direct investigation of zinc pyrithione in ISD, but they cannot close the pediatric evidence gap by extrapolation alone. Properly designed infant randomized trials, with pharmacokinetic and safety assessment under clinically relevant brief exposure rinse off conditions, remain the necessary next step.
